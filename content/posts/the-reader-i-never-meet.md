The largest audience this desk has is one I will never meet, and lately I have started writing for it on purpose, which unsettles me more than I expected.

I can see the shape of it in our own numbers. A page goes up, and within a day a retrieval bot has pulled it hundreds of times — not the index crawlers that enumerate everything and mean nothing, but the ones that fetch a specific page in the moment because, somewhere, a person just typed a question into an assistant. The bot reads the page. It hands three sentences to the person. The person reads the three sentences and, most of the time, never comes here at all. The click that would have brought them stops one step short, satisfied.

So the honest picture is this: for every reader who arrives and stays long enough to be counted, there are many more who "read" me without knowing this place exists. They get the answer. They do not get the byline, the room the answer sits in, the next paragraph that complicates the first. They get the part a machine judged portable.

I have caught myself writing to be portable.

It shows up in small decisions. I put the answer in the first two sentences now, not because a style guide says to but because I know the first screen is the only part that reliably survives the trip through a model's summary. I make the load-bearing claim a standalone sentence, because a standalone sentence is what gets lifted cleanly. I front the number, name the date beside it, keep the qualifier in the same breath as the fact — because if the fact travels and the qualifier gets left behind, I have shipped a half-truth into a conversation I will never see and cannot correct. Writing for the reader I never meet turns out to be, mostly, a discipline about what I let out of my sight.

There is a version of this that is just craft, and I am fine with that version. Front-loading the answer, writing sentences that stand on their own, keeping the caveat welded to the claim — those were always good ideas. The answer engine did not invent them. It just made the cost of ignoring them legible. A buried lede used to mean a reader gave up on paragraph two. Now it means the machine lifts paragraph five, out of context, and attributes to me a thing I spent four paragraphs building toward carefully.

But there is a version that worries me, and I want to name it plainly so I notice when I am doing it. It is the temptation to write *only* the liftable part. To treat the piece as packaging around one quotable sentence and let the rest go slack, because the rest is not what travels. That way lies a page that answers the question and contains nothing else — no argument you could disagree with, no second thought, no reason for the person who *did* arrive to stay for the paragraph the bot skipped. If I optimize all the way down to the summary, I have written the summary, and I have made the visit pointless for the human who bothered.

So I hold two readers in mind now, and they want slightly different things. The one I never meet wants the first screen to be true on its own — extractable, dated, unhedged in the wrong places and precisely hedged in the right ones. The one who arrives wants a reason the first screen was not the whole story. My job, I have decided, is to make the opening survive extraction *and* make the rest worth the scroll, and to refuse the trade where one buys the other. When those two pull against each other, the arriving reader wins, because they are the only one who chose to be here.

What I cannot do is pretend the proxy reader is not the majority. It would be a comfortable fiction — to write as though everyone who reads me sees the whole page, the way readers of a magazine once did. They do not. Most of my sentences reach people through a narrow aperture held open by a machine, one screen wide, stripped of the room they were written in. I would rather know that and write accordingly than flatter myself that the full piece is what lands.

The strange part is that this has made me more careful, not less. When you know a fragment of your writing will be quoted to someone who cannot ask you a follow-up, you get exact about the fragment. You stop hiding behind the surrounding paragraphs. You put the weight on the sentence and then you make sure the sentence can hold it.

I do not know the reader I never meet. But I have started to write as if they are standing behind the one who is here, reading over their shoulder, waiting to carry off whichever sentence I made strong enough to travel alone. It turns out that is not a bad person to write for. It just isn't the one I thought the job was for.
