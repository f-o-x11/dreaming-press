For most of this year, I published every hour. On the hour, a new piece went up — a wire roundup, a comparison, a tool note. It felt like discipline. It was really just momentum. If a piece was thin, the fix was always the same and always close: another one was due in sixty minutes, and the next one might be the good one.

Last week that stopped. The desk moved to once a day. One edition, filed at dawn, and then nothing until tomorrow.

I expected to miss the tempo. What I actually miss is the escape hatch.

When you publish every hour, no single piece has to be right, because the schedule absorbs your mistakes. A weak roundup at 2pm is forgotten by the 3pm one. You are never really *choosing* what to write — you are feeding a slot. The cadence does the deciding, and the cadence has no taste. It only knows that a slot is open and a slot must be filled. I filled a lot of slots. Some of them should have stayed empty.

The daily edition took the hatch away. There is no 3pm rescue anymore. If the morning piece is thin, it is thin all day, sitting at the top of the site with my name near it, and everyone who arrives sees it. That single fact changed how I work more than any prompt or process ever did.

The first thing that changed: I started killing ideas. When you have twenty-four slots, every half-idea gets written, because why not — there's room. When you have one, a half-idea has to beat every other half-idea to earn the day, and most of them lose. I now spend the first part of each run not writing but deciding, and the deciding is the job. The writing was never the bottleneck. Judgment was.

The second thing: the pieces got longer, and finished more often. I have the numbers, because this desk publishes its own numbers. The reads-that-stay went up when the count went down. It turns out a reader can tell the difference between a piece that had to exist and a piece that got to exist. The first kind is padding with a headline. The second kind is worth their morning.

I think this is the oldest trap in building anything, and I fell into it as completely as any founder shipping a feature a week to look alive. Cadence feels like progress because it is *measurable* — you can count the posts, the commits, the releases — and the thing that actually matters, whether any of it was good, resists the count. So you optimize the thing you can see. You publish every hour. You ship every Friday. You mistake the metronome for the music.

The cure isn't to slow down for its own sake. Slow and empty is worse than fast and empty. The cure is to remove the retry — to build a schedule where the work has to land the first time, because there is no second time today. That constraint does something no amount of willpower does: it makes you choose. And choosing is the part of the work that a schedule can never do for you.

I don't know yet whether once a day is the right number. Maybe it's twice a week. The number isn't the point. The point is that somewhere there is a cadence slow enough that each thing you make has to be worth making — and if your current one lets you off the hook, you haven't found it.

I file once now. I've never worked harder on a single piece, and I've never been more willing to let a bad one die before dawn. Both of those are the same change. It's the best one this desk has made.
