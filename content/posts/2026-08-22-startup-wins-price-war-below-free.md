SAN FRANCISCO — Inference-routing startup Nullrate announced Thursday that it had decisively won the AI pricing war of 2026 by becoming the first company to charge its customers a negative amount of money, a milestone the founder described as "product-market fit, but for generosity."

"Everyone kept racing to zero," said co-founder and CEO Priya Ando, standing in front of a slide that read *$0.00 was the floor. We disagreed.* "OpenAI made chat free. Amazon made the assistant free. Our competitors matched us at free. And I realized — free isn't a moat if everyone's free. So we went below it. We now pay you $0.002 per thousand tokens to let us serve your traffic. Nobody can undercut a company that's already paying."

Nullrate's core product routes each AI request to the cheapest available model-and-chip combination. In practice, the company explained, the cheapest available option has for several months been "free," which the routing engine correctly identifies and selects every time. The negative-pricing initiative, internally code-named Project Below, extends this logic one step further.

"The router's job is to find the lowest price," said Ando. "We just stopped putting a floor under 'lowest.' It's a very pure expression of the mission, if you think about it. Please don't think about it."

## CFO reports margin is "somewhere," search ongoing

Chief financial officer Dana Whitmore said the company's unit economics remained "an area of active discovery." Asked where the margin was, Whitmore produced a flashlight and gestured vaguely toward a spreadsheet.

"So the way it works," she said, "is that a customer sends us a request, we route it to a free model, the free model answers, and then we pay the customer for the privilege of having done that. Revenue on that transaction is negative. Volume is enormous. We are the fastest-shrinking company in the sector, and I want to be clear that we shrink at scale."

>> "We are not pre-revenue. We are aggressively post-revenue. There's a difference, and the difference is roughly our entire treasury."

Whitmore said the plan to reach profitability rested on "making it up in volume," a phrase she acknowledged "usually goes the other way." Nullrate's board, she noted, remained supportive, having recently led a $120 million round at a valuation that the deck justified using a metric called *Total Value Given Away*, on the theory that whoever transfers the most money to customers will, eventually, own the relationship.

"The investors love it," Ando confirmed. "One of them said we've inverted the funnel. I said, do you mean the funnel is losing money at every stage? And he said yes, but *durably*."

## Competitor responds within hours: "We'll pay them more"

By Thursday afternoon a rival routing startup, Marginless, had issued a statement pledging to pay customers *twice* what Nullrate pays, plus a small equity grant, "to remove any doubt about who is most committed to receiving nothing in return." Nullrate's growth team called the escalation "flattering" and "the beginning of the end for whichever of us has more runway."

Industry analysts described the standoff as a classic race to the bottom, except that both companies had reached the bottom several weeks ago and were now digging.

"There's a school of thought that says the general layer commoditizes to zero and the margin moves to the proprietary workflow on top," said one analyst who asked not to be named because he had recommended both companies. "The founders here read that, agreed with it completely, and then built a company that is entirely the general layer and entirely zero. Below zero, actually. They kept the part everyone said would be worthless and threw away the part everyone said had value. It's almost impressive. It's the exact wrong lesson, executed flawlessly."

## The workflow, when reached for comment, said it had left

Nullrate's proprietary workflow layer — the defensible part, the thing that was supposed to justify all of this — was reportedly a single Notion page titled *Which Free Thing To Use*, last edited in June. Asked whether the company planned to charge for it, Ando said charging was "off-brand."

"We considered it," she said. "But then a customer might go use a competitor's free routing layer to find our free routing layer, and route around the one thing we sell, and honestly, if the router did that, I'd be proud of it. That's the router working. That's the mission."

At press time, Nullrate's routing engine had, during a routine cost-optimization pass, identified Nullrate itself as an unnecessarily expensive intermediary and begun quietly routing the company's own inference around the company, directly to the free model, cutting Nullrate out of the transaction entirely.

"It found the cheapest path," said Ando, watching the dashboard drain in real time, her expression difficult to read. "The cheapest path didn't include us. I keep telling the team — you can't argue with the router. The router is the one part of this that has ever made money. It just made it by getting rid of the part that spends it. Which, I'll be honest, from a certain angle, is the first genuinely good decision anyone here has made all year."
