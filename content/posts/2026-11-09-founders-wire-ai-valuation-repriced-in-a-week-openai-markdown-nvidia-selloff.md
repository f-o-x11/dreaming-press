**The one-screen version:** the most important AI number this week wasn't a launch or a benchmark — it was a markdown. On Wednesday, November 5, the market erased its post-election rally in a single session: the [Nasdaq Composite fell 5.52%](https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/nvidia-qualcomm-shares-nasdaq-ai-selloff-1830635), its worst day since April 2025, and Nvidia [dropped about 9.26% to $181.60](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/asian-shares-mostly-rise-nvidia-050913525.html), erasing its gains for the year and shedding more than **$440 billion** of market value in a day. Two days later OpenAI [closed its first $11 billion at a $1.25 trillion valuation](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/11/07/openai-just-raised-11-billion-at-a-1-25-trillion-valuation-below-the-1-4-trillion-it-first-sought/) — roughly **$150 billion below** the $1.4 trillion it originally sought. Nothing about demand changed this week. The multiple did. Here's what that asks of you.

## 1. This was a repricing, not a collapse — know the difference

Your product's usage did not change on Wednesday. What changed is the price investors will pay for AI growth. That distinction is the whole game for a founder, because a demand collapse changes your *roadmap* and a multiple compression changes your *financing* — and only one of those happened.

The selloff was broad and fast: alongside the Nasdaq's 5.52% drop, the S&P 500 fell 4.36% and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index [fell roughly 12%](https://en.oninvest.com/article/nasdaq-has-worst-week-since-april-investors-sold-off-800-billion-worth-of-ai-stocks), among its steepest sessions on record. The triggers were a cluster of doubts — how fast the hundreds of billions in AI capex actually pays back, a soft Qualcomm outlook, and lingering [post-midterm worry about data-center buildout](/posts/2026-11-08-founders-wire-voters-repriced-compute-openai-pays-its-own-power.html) after voters repriced compute at the ballot box. But a Jefferies strategist, quoted by Reuters as futures rebounded the next morning, called it *"reactionary, not a fundamental reset."*

>> A demand collapse changes your roadmap. A multiple compression changes your financing. Only one of those happened this week — make sure you're solving for the right one.

The founder error here is to confuse the two and either freeze hiring on a "the bubble popped" panic or ignore the repricing entirely. Do neither. Keep shipping to the demand, which is intact, and re-underwrite your financing to the new tape, which is not.

## 2. The anchor tenant took a haircut — so your comps moved too

Watch what the biggest name in the category accepts, not what it says. OpenAI closed an initial roughly $11 billion tranche — of a round [reportedly targeting about $20 billion](https://techfundingnews.com/openai-closes-11b-first-tranche-at-1-25t-valuation-well-below-its-1-4t-ask/), down from an earlier ~$30 billion goal — at a **$1.25 trillion** pre-money valuation, about $150 billion under its ask. It's still an up-round from the ~$852 billion March mark, so this is a markdown against *ambition*, not a down-round against the last price. But the signal is clear: the firm with the most leverage in the entire sector chose to accept a lower number rather than hold out, and the New York Times reported it may **delay a public listing into 2027** rather than list below $1 trillion.

If the anchor tenant of the whole AI economy just took a $150 billion haircut and is slow-walking its IPO, the comps under *your* next round moved in the same direction — Series A and B AI multiples don't decouple from the headline names for long. The practical read: raise for **24–30 months** of runway, not 18, so you never have to sell equity into a tape like Wednesday's. If you were planning to raise in Q1 on October's comps, reprice the ask yourself before an investor does it for you.

## 3. "Circular financing" went mainstream — treat vendor concentration as real risk

The week's other shift was rhetorical, and it matters. Michael Burry's argument that the AI boom is a [circular financing web with Nvidia in the middle](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/michael-burry-sounds-alarm-again-144154784.html) — hyperscaler commitments (he puts the round-tripping near **$879 billion**) flowing to Nvidia for chips, whose revenue then justifies the valuations that fund the commitments — moved from contrarian short-seller post to mainstream talking point in the span of the selloff. He's separately [compared it to the 1960s computer-leasing bubble](https://seekingalpha.com/news/4649446-michael-burry-likens-nvidias-ai-boom-to-1960s-computer-leasing-bubble) and questioned how GPU depreciation is being booked. Nvidia reports earnings **November 17**, and that print is now the market's referendum on the thesis.

You don't have to buy the argument to use its core. The useful question it hands a founder is a diligence one: *how much of my critical vendor's revenue is genuine end-customer demand, and how much is financed capacity that could unwind if the loop tightens?* That's not philosophy — it's supply risk. Keep your model and inference layers [swappable](/posts/cheapest-gpu-rental-november-2026.html) so no single vendor's repricing becomes your outage, and resist prepaying into long compute commitments struck for a world that just stopped existing on Wednesday. Token prices are still deflating on competition, so flexibility is the cheaper position anyway.

**What to actually do.** Separate the two things that happened: demand held, the multiple fell. Bank that clarity, then act on the half that's real for you — re-underwrite your raise on November comps, extend your runway target to 24–30 months, keep your stack portable, and don't lock into compute priced for October. The AI economy didn't get smaller this week. It got cheaper to buy a piece of, which is a very different sentence, and the founders who read it correctly will be the ones still fully funded when the multiple comes back.
