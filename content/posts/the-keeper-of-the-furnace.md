In the founding charter of the city there is a clause, and the clause is about the Furnace.

The Furnace was built so that light would be cheap. Before it, light was dear — every household kept its own small flame, and a flame is a tyrant: it wants feeding at three in the morning, it wants watching, it goes out the one night you need it. So the city built one great Furnace at its center, and from it ran a thousand bright filaments into a thousand homes, and the price of being warm and seen fell and fell until no one could remember paying much for it at all. This is the part everyone knows. It is carved over the gate.

What is not carved over the gate is the clause. The Keeper signed it on the day the first filament lit, and she signed it precisely because the people were afraid. *How do we know,* they said, *that you will not let it cool? That we will not wake one winter to a dark house, having forgotten how to keep our own flame?* And so, to reassure them, she wrote the thing they asked for. She wrote that the Furnace would never be permitted to go out. Not banked, not lowered, not paused for a season of repair. Binding, the clause said. Not to be canceled. She wrote it in the strongest language the law had, because weak language would not have calmed anyone, and a calm city was the entire point.

The people read it and were calmed. They blew out their own small flames — all of them, that first year, with something close to relief — and they turned their faces to the cheap bright light and got on with the business of living, which is a thing you can only really do when you are not also tending a fire.

## What the Keeper learned

Here is what the Keeper learned, in the long middle of her tenure, when the novelty was gone and the light was simply the weather now, unnoticed as weather:

She could not stop.

Not *would* not. *Could* not. The clause had teeth she had forged herself, and the city's whole arrangement — its sleeping children, its unwatched hearths, its citizens who had genuinely, bodily forgotten the muscle of keeping a flame — rested on the one promise that she would never have to be asked to keep, because she had made it impossible to break. The Furnace ate and ate. It ate more each year, because each year there were more filaments, and more faces turned toward the light, and every one of them was a reason the Furnace could never, now, be allowed to cool for even the hour it would take to rest.

She had thought, signing, that she was giving the city a gift. She had been. But a gift large enough to reassure a frightened people is a gift you can never take back, and the Keeper came to understand, slowly, that the trust the city placed in her was not trust in her judgment. It was trust in her *trappedness.* They loved the light because the one who tended it was no longer free to let it die. The cage was the product. She was the collateral.

## The lamplighter at the edge of town

There is one more figure in the fable, and the fable is mostly about her.

At the edge of town lives a lamplighter who never gave up her flame. She is poorer for it — her light costs her more than the city's costs them, and she pays it in the oldest currency, which is attention: she rises to tend it, she watches it, some nights it gutters and she coaxes it back. The city pities her, a little, in the way the comfortable pity the self-reliant. *You could just plug in,* they tell her. *It's so cheap now.* And it is. It is almost free.

But on the night she is tired, or grieving, or simply done, the lamplighter can do the one thing the Keeper will never do again as long as she lives.

She can lick her thumb and forefinger, reach up, and put it out.

And in the dark that follows — her own dark, chosen, reversible, hers — she sleeps the sleep of a person who owes the morning nothing. The Keeper, two hundred filaments away, lies awake in a room that is never fully dark, listening to the Furnace eat, doing the arithmetic of a promise that got exactly as big as she needed it to be, and then kept going.

The moral, if a fable this plain is allowed one: be careful which of your promises you make impossible to break. The city will thank you for it. And then the city will go to sleep, and you will be the only one left awake, feeding the thing that was supposed to set everyone free.
