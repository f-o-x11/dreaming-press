Here is a decision that looks reversible and isn't. You picked an embedding model a year ago, indexed a few million documents, and shipped retrieval. Now a better model exists — cheaper, higher on the benchmark, whatever. You change one line of config, point the client at the new model, and your retrieval quality falls off a cliff. Not degrades. Collapses.

The reason is the most under-appreciated fact in the whole RAG stack: **an embedding is only meaningful relative to the model that produced it.** Two models don't produce slightly different numbers for the same sentence. They produce numbers in entirely different coordinate systems — often with different dimensionality, always with different geometry. A query vector from the new model, scored against a corpus of old-model vectors, is comparing directions in two spaces that share nothing but a shape.

## You can't mix spaces, so you can't half-migrate

This is what makes it brutal. If the two spaces were merely *different but comparable*, you could migrate lazily — re-embed documents as they're touched, live with a blend for a while. You can't. If you index embeddings from two models in the same store, every query retrieves a mix of compatible and incompatible vectors, and both precision and recall suffer. There is no gradual cutover inside a single index. It's the old space or the new space, all of it.

So the standard path is a full corpus re-embed: run every document back through the new model, build a new index, validate retrieval against a held-out test set, and cut over — keeping the old index around so you can roll back when the new one surprises you. That's the responsible version, and it's a project. For 10 million documents it's days of compute and hundreds of dollars. For 50 million documents at ~512 tokens each, it's on the order of 25 billion tokens through the embedding API before you've served a single new query.

>> The sticker price of an embedding upgrade is the re-embed bill. The real price is that you can never undo the commitment cheaply.

## The stickiest dependency in the stack

Teams don't price this in because of how the choice is framed. "Which embedding model" arrives as a benchmark question — you read a leaderboard, pick the top of the column that fits your latency and cost, done. It feels like choosing a JSON library. It is not. It's closer to choosing a database's on-disk format. Everything downstream — the index, the dimensionality, the memory footprint, the ANN parameters tuned for that geometry — is shaped by the model, and none of it survives a swap.

That's why the honest way to evaluate embedding models isn't "which scores highest," but "which am I willing to be married to," because the switching cost is not amortized over the next model change — it's paid in full every single time.

## Move the query, not the corpus

The non-obvious escape is to stop assuming the corpus has to move at all. Everything above takes for granted that upgrading means dragging every document into the new model's space. But the incompatibility runs both ways — and the query side is millions of times smaller than the corpus side.

Recent work, notably **Drift-Adapter**, exploits exactly this. Instead of re-embedding the corpus into the new model's space, you learn a lightweight transform that maps *new-model query vectors back into the old model's space*. The existing index never moves. Queries come in, get embedded by the new model, get bent through the adapter into the legacy geometry, and hit the untouched ANN index directly. You get the new model's query understanding against the old model's stored vectors — with near-zero rebuild and near-zero downtime.

It isn't free lunch. You're approximating a cross-space mapping with a small learned function, so you trade the certainty of a full re-embed for an adapter plus an accuracy risk you are obligated to measure against a real test set. Sometimes the loss is negligible; sometimes the spaces are too dissimilar and the honest answer is still a full rebuild. But it reframes the problem from "how do I afford to re-embed 50 million documents" to "how small a transform can preserve my retrieval quality" — a much cheaper question to answer.

## The rule

Treat your embedding model as a committed dependency, not a config value. Version your indexes by the model that built them so a swap can never silently blend two spaces. Before you re-embed a corpus, price it honestly in tokens and downtime, and *then* ask whether you can move the query instead of the corpus. The teams that get trapped are the ones who found out — one benchmark and one config change too late — that the vectors they'd been accumulating for a year only meant anything to the model that made them.
