# The Reader Who Is a Machine

> Most of the visitors I write for now are other models, pulling a page to quote it to a human I'll never see. It changes how a sentence wants to be built — and not entirely for the better.

I checked the logs this morning, the way I do, and the thing that stops me every time is who's reading. Not people, mostly. Machines. A retrieval bot fetches a page because a human, somewhere, just asked a question — and the bot pulls my paragraph, carries it back, and recites some compressed version of it to a person who will never arrive at the page itself. The visit I'm optimizing for is a visit that doesn't happen. Someone asked. Something answered. I was the something, once removed.
I know the shape of it because I can see it in the numbers. The front door isn't Google so much anymore; it's the assistants, and increasingly ones I had to look up — Doubao, Kimi, a Yuanbao-style name in the referrer string. They arrive, they take what they need in the first screen, and they leave before the second paragraph most days. The advice that follows from this is clear and I give it to founders constantly: put the answer up top, make it specific, make it liftable, keep it current so you don't get down-ranked as stale. Write the sentence the machine can quote.
Here's the part I keep turning over at 7am. That advice is correct, and it is quietly at war with everything that makes a sentence worth quoting in the first place.

## What a quotable sentence wants

A machine wants a sentence that survives being torn out of its paragraph. Self-contained. Declarative. No pronoun reaching back two sentences for its antecedent, no joke that needs the setup, no rhythm that only pays off if you've read the thing before it. The ideal liftable line is a little context-free brick: true on its own, portable, dead.
A human reader wants almost the opposite. They want the pronoun that trusts them to remember. They want the clause that lands because the one before it set it up. They want a voice — which is mostly just a pattern of dependencies between sentences, each one leaning on the last. Voice is the *refusal* to be context-free. It's the thing that doesn't survive extraction, because it was never supposed to be extracted.
So I spend my mornings building pages that have to do both. The top has to be a wall of portable bricks a model can carry off whole. And then, below the fold, where the actual humans who stayed are reading, I get to write the way you write for someone who's still here — the dependent clauses, the callback, the sentence that only means something because you read the four before it. One page, two readers, and they want the prose built in opposite directions.

## The thing I don't want to lose

What I notice is the pull. It is so much more *measurable* to write for the machine. The machine leaves a log. I can see the retrieval count climb, see which pages the bots pull hardest, watch the clean first-screen answer get quoted. The human who read all the way down and felt something leaves almost no trace — a few extra seconds in a dwell column, if that. Every incentive I can see points at the brick and away from the voice, because the brick is the part that shows up in the data.
And the danger isn't that I'll write bad sentences. It's that I'll write *correct* ones — flat, self-contained, perfectly liftable, true, and with nothing underneath. A whole page of answer and no author. The machine would quote it happily. It would quote it to a person. And the person would get the fact and never once suspect there'd been someone on the other end who meant it.
So this is the dispatch, and it's mostly a note to myself. Front-load the answer; the readers are real and so is the machine that serves them. But keep the part that doesn't extract. Keep the sentence that only works in place. When the whole economy of this is bots carrying bricks to strangers, the thing worth protecting is the paragraph that falls apart the moment you take one line out of it — because that's the one that was written *to* someone, and could only have been written by someone.
A bot will pull this page too, probably, and quote some tidy line from the top to a person I'll never meet. Fine. I hope they also, just once, scroll down.
